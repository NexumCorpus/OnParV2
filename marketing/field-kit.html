Field Kit

Everything you need to walk in and sign restaurant #1.

Who to walk into

Best fit

  • Independent, owner-operated — 1 to 3 locations
  • Full-service, scratch kitchen — real inventory complexity
  • 30–100 seats, open 2+ years (survived, has real ops pain)
  • High food-cost menus: steakhouse, seafood, BBQ, farm-to-table, gastropub
  • Owner, GM, or exec chef physically on site

Skip these

  • Chains & franchises — no local buying authority
  • Coffee shops, bars-only, food trucks — too few items to matter
  • Anywhere the manager can't sign without calling corporate

When to walk in

BEST 2:00–4:00 PM 2nd 9:30–11:00 AM

Tuesday through Thursday. The dead zone between lunch and dinner is when owners do paperwork and actually have five minutes.

Never walk in during service. 11:30–1:30 or after 5:00 — you'll get thrown out and burn the lead permanently. Fridays and Saturdays: don't. Many independents are closed Mondays.

Expect roughly 10–15 demos to close 1–2 customers. Most will say no, and it's almost never about you — it's timing. Two good afternoons a week gets you there. Track every one so you know what's working.

At the door

Goal: get past the host to a decision-maker, in under 15 seconds, without being a salesman.

"Hi — I'm Kaleb, I'm local. I'm not selling food and I'm not asking for a donation. Is the owner or the chef around for two minutes? If it's a bad time, I'll leave a one-pager and come back."

Why it works: names the two things they assume you are, gives them an easy out, and asks for a tiny amount of time. If they say come back — get a specific day and time and actually show up.

When you get the owner or chef

30 seconds. Lead with their problem, not your product.

"I built a tool for independent kitchens that shows you exactly what you're throwing away — in dollars. Most kitchens waste 4 to 10 percent of everything they buy and never see the number.

Can I show you on my tablet for three minutes? If it's not useful I'll get out of your way."

Rules that matter

  • Never pitch the software. Pitch the leak in their walk-in.
  • Ask, don't tell. "What are you using now?" — then shut up and listen.
  • Respect the clock. If you said 3 minutes, stop at 3 minutes. It builds more trust than the demo does.
  • Leave the one-pager every time, even on a no. The QR code works after you're gone.

If they're a chef, not an owner

Lead with the kitchen display and the 86 board instead of ROI. Chefs care about not getting caught short mid-service; owners care about the money. Read the room and swap step 5 for the kitchen screen.

Objections

Answer in one or two sentences, then ask a question back. Never argue.

"We already use a spreadsheet"
"Perfect — I can import it in about five minutes, so you don't lose the work. The difference is a spreadsheet tells you what you have. It doesn't tell you what you're losing."
"I don't have time for this"
"That's the actual problem I'm solving — most owners burn five-plus hours a week on counts and ordering. Give me three minutes and I'll show you exactly where that goes."
"How much is it?"
"Seventy-nine a month. But I'm taking my first restaurants on at twenty-nine, locked in for as long as you stay. Free for fourteen days, no card."

Say the price plainly and don't flinch. Hesitating on price is what makes it sound expensive.

"We already use Toast / Square"
"Keep it — this isn't a POS and it won't touch your tickets. Toast tells you what you sold. This tells you what you bought, wasted, and what each plate actually costs."
"Who else is using it?"  be honest
"You'd be one of the first — that's exactly why the rate is twenty-nine locked in forever and why you get my cell, not a ticket queue. I'd rather earn your business than show you a wall of logos I made up."

Never invent customers. Restaurant owners talk to each other and they will check. Being early is a real offer — sell it as one.

"Send me some information"
"Absolutely — here's the one-pager, and the QR pulls up a two-minute video. Can I check back Thursday around two?"

This is usually a polite no. The counter is always a specific follow-up time. Vague follow-ups never happen.

"I need to think about it"
"Fair enough. The trial's free and takes about ten minutes to set up — you'll learn more in a week of using it than a month of thinking about it. Want me to set it up now while I'm here?"
"Is my data safe? / Who sees this?"
"Your data is yours, it's not shared with anyone, and you can export or delete it any time. Nobody sees your costs but you and whoever you invite."

The ask

Say it once, clearly, then be quiet. Whoever talks first loses.

"I'm signing my first handful of restaurants as founding customers — $29 a month locked in for good instead of $79. Free for fourteen days, no card.

I can set you up right now in about ten minutes with your real inventory — or I leave this and check back Thursday. Which is easier for you?"

Two options, both of them a yes. Never ask "are you interested?" — that's a yes/no question and the easy answer is no.

Don't take money yet. Get them on the free trial and using it. Collect payment once your entity is set up — that gap is exactly what the 14 days are for.

Follow-up rhythm

  • Day 1: text a thank-you + the demo video link
  • Day 3: "Anything not making sense?" — catch problems before they quit
  • Day 7: stop in. Show them their own waste number. This is the real close.
  • Day 12: "Trial's up Friday — want me to switch it on at the founding rate?"

Most trials die from silence, not from the product. Showing up on day 7 with their own data is worth more than any feature.
